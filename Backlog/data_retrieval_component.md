# FIRST COMPONENT: Data Source and Retrieval Plan

## OpenAlex as the database for retrieve PA records

### Why OpenAlex is a Suitable and Reliable Source for Bibliometric Analyses

OpenAlex is a non-profit, open, and continuously expanding scholarly database built on an entity-based structure connecting works, authors, institutions, sources, and funders (Priem et al., 2022).
Developed as the continuation and expansion of the Microsoft Academic Graph—except for patents (Culbert et al. 2025)—it provides free programmatic access to bibliographic metadata.

### Coverage and Comparative Performance

A growing body of studies positions OpenAlex as a strong alternative to traditional proprietary databases.
Alperin et al. (2024) list numerous works already using OpenAlex for bibliometric, altmetric, and scoping-review purposes. Their comparative analysis shows that OpenAlex outperforms Scopus in coverage while presenting minor weaknesses in metadata completeness—such as publication dates, volume and issue numbers, page information, abstracts, author names, or institutional affiliations. They conclude that OpenAlex can already serve as a reliable basis for some forms of bibliometric analysis.
Thelwall and Jiang (2025) similarly find that OpenAlex delivers citation counts comparable to—or in some fields greater than—Scopus, confirming its suitability for bibliometric and evaluative research.Their results suggest that Scopus may still maintain slightly higher precision in citation data owing to its maturity, but OpenAlex achieves broader document coverage.

Culbert et al. (2025) provide further empirical validation through an internal coverage comparison, measuring the proportion of cited references in a dataset that are themselves indexed as source items. They demonstrate that OpenAlex performs comparably to WoS and Scopus in this respect and can thus be used as a credible substitute for large-scale analyses. They note, however, that because OpenAlex records only source references—citations to works already indexed—it may underestimate total citation counts relative to databases containing “non-source references” (final section).

Vieira and Leta (2024) reinforce the importance of coverage measures such as overlaps (the proportion of items shared across databases) to evaluate database comprehensiveness. This methodological logic underpins much of Culbert et al.’s work, as they argue that OpenAlex performs well when assessed via internal coverage but still differs from Scopus and WoS in document representation.

### Metadata Quality and Known Limitations

While generally robust, OpenAlex metadata still present limitations acknowledged by several studies.
Alperin et al. (2024) identify issues in the correctness of bibliographic information and document-type classification. Thelwall and Jiang (2025) also note the misclassification of editorial contributions as journal articles, which may distort field-normalised citation metrics. Rodrigues et al. (2025) discuss errors that can prevent citation matching—such as inconsistent or incorrect DOIs, author names, or journal titles. They warn that relying on a single source can produce incomplete data, either because of coverage limitations or citation misidentifications.
Delgado-Quirós and Ortega (2024) highlight similar concerns but point out that quality assessment must go beyond quantity, considering the accuracy of content and metadata. They report that OpenAlex performs well on overall reliability, though early versions showed losses in bibliographic details such as volume or page number. Nonetheless, since its 2023 release, OpenAlex has undergone multiple rounds of improvement documented in its public release notes (OurResearch, 2025), such as the launch of Walden—the full rewrite of OpenAlex with 190M new works and cleaner metadata.

### Transparency, Accessibility, and Open-Science Alignment

One of OpenAlex’s strongest assets is its non-profit and open-access orientation. Alperin et al. (2024) stress that its open data and source code make it a transparent infrastructure. Haunschild and Bornmann (2024) praise its free accessibility and replicability, describing OpenAlex as essential for building transparent and reusable science maps. Scheidsteger et al. (2025) situate OpenAlex within a broader movement of open research infrastructures (ORIDs), arguing that transparent, auditable databases are vital for democratising research evaluation and ensuring that assessments can be reproduced by anyone, not only by those with access to proprietary systems.

### Suitability for the Present Project

Given these characteristics, OpenAlex is an appropriate and reliable choice for the Public Administration Snapshot project.
1. Comprehensive coverage: Alperin et al. (2024) show that it functions as a superset of Scopus, ensuring a wide inclusion of Public Administration works.
2. Transparent infrastructure: its open API and documented updates enable reproducible workflows (Alperin et al. 2024; Haunschild & Bornmann 2024).
3. Proven utility: multiple comparative studies (Culbert et al. 2025; Thelwall & Jiang 2025; Scheidsteger et al. 2025) confirm its growing suitability for bibliometric and evaluative research.
At the same time, metadata inconsistencies (Alperin et al. 2024; Delgado-Quirós & Ortega 2024), misclassifications (Thelwall & Jiang 2025), and missing references (Culbert et al. 2025) require careful handling through transparent cleaning and disambiguation, which this project explicitly documents. However, the new release of Walden, the full rewrite of OpenAlex, seems promising for what concern metadata overall quality and precision. 

### Conclusion

Across the literature, OpenAlex emerges as a credible, improving, and ethically aligned bibliometric data source. Its combination of broad coverage, open accessibility, and commitment to continuous improvement supports its use as a foundation for reproducible research. By acknowledging its limitations and applying systematic data-cleaning procedures, projects such as this one can leverage OpenAlex’s openness while ensuring scientific rigour and traceability.

### Boundaries of the records
- General difficulty in defining a field
Under the umbrella of "metascience" we could find many disciplines that have science as their object of study—philosophy of science, science of science, sociology of science, economy of science, history of science, scientometrics, etc. A fundamental question for these sciences has been, and still is, how to define the conceptual boundaries of a discipline and how to find the adequate operationalizations for specific purposes. **Sugimoto and Larivière 2018** 
Defining what constitutes a scientific field or discipline is notoriously difficult. Scholars frequently disagree on the boundaries of fields because these boundaries are neither fixed nor purely intellectual. A main obstacle in defining a field is because is often confused with partially overlapping notions such as field, specialty, research area, or scientific domain. These terms reflect different levels of granularity, historical traditions, and institutional arrangements, which makes a single stable definition elusive **Sugimoto and Weingart 2015**. Furthermore, what counts as a discipline is not merely an academic category: it carries symbolic and economic consequences. Indeed, declaring that a scientific area “is” or “is not” a discipline influences university structures, funding flows, evaluation metrics, and scholarly identities, making the act of definition itself part of a broader struggle over power and legitimacy within the scientific system **Bourdieu 2004**. For these reasons, **Boyack and Klavans 2011** caution that existing disciplinary classifications are highly useful for **search and retrieval**, but should be employed with care when they are taken as the basis for science metrics or normative judgements about quality. 

- Multiple Conceptualisations and Definitions
Given this complexity, scholars have proposed a wide range of conceptualisations of disciplines and fields. **Sugimoto and Weingart 2015** group these into several themes: **cognitive definitions** emphasise shared problems, theories, and methods; **social definitions** highlight organised communities and shared norms; **communicative definitions** focus on specialised terminologies and discourse communities; definitions based on **separatedness** stress identity and boundary-making; others foreground **tradition, continuity, or institutionalisation** through departments, curricula, and journals. These dimensions coexist in different combinations across fields, resulting in diverse and sometimes contradictory understandings. Contemporary scholarship also cautions that disciplinary boundaries have become increasingly flexible under conditions of specialisation and interdisciplinarity, making rigid definitions even harder to sustain **Eykens 2022**. Overall, disciplines emerge as multi-dimensional constructs shaped by intellectual, social, communicative, and institutional factors.

- Defining a Field: A Bourdieusian Perspective
In this project, I define a scientific field through a Bourdieusian lens, as a **relational social space** in which agents and institutions compete over the production, validation, and distribution of legitimate knowledge. A field is not simply a collection of topics or a list of journals; it is a structured space of positions and position-takings, shaped by historically sedimented **power relations**, **struggles**, and **interests** **Bourdieu 1975**. What is at stake in these struggles is the very **definition of science** in that domain: which problems, methods, theories, and standards of evidence are recognised as properly “scientific”, and which are marginal, heterodox, or excluded. This aligns with the definition provided by **Wagner et al. 2011** about disciplines, in which the authors focused more on the cognitive side of a discipline, abiding more to a more Mertonian definition of discipline.
Central to this perspective is the role of **capital**. Scientific fields are structured by the unequal distribution of **economic capital** (funding, positions, material resources) and, crucially, **symbolic capital**. Symbolic capital is a second-order form of capital that rests on recognition and legitimacy; it is composed of **cultural capital** (the specific knowledge, concepts, methods and skills recognised as valuable in the field) and **social capital** (the networks, collaborations, institutional connections, and reputational ties among agents) **Bourdieu 1975; Schirone 2023**. These forms of capital are not merely descriptive: they shape who can speak with authority, which journals and institutions are seen as central, and which contributions acquire visibility and impact. This symbolic capital, called by Bourdieu **scientific authority**, is a specific configuration of cultural and social capital that allows certain agents or institutions to act as legitimate spokespersons for the field **Bourdieu 1975**.
As the locus for the struggle for scientific capital, a field is therefore **simultaneously cognitive and social**. On the cognitive side, it is defined by a moving frontier of legitimate problems, theories, and methods—what counts as a meaningful question, a proper explanation, or an acceptable piece of evidence. On the social side, it is constituted by communities, institutions, and hierarchies that stabilise and reproduce these cognitive structures over time: departments, journals, editorial boards, professional associations, degree programmes, and research centres. The intellectual organisation of the field and its social organisation are mutually constitutive: recognised concepts and theories help shape who is admitted, promoted, or marginalised; conversely, the distribution of positions and power conditions which kinds of knowledge are likely to be produced and consecrated **Schirone 2023; Eykens 2022**.
Bourdieu’s notion of **habitus** deepens this picture. Habitus refers to the set of conscious and unconscious dispositions through which agents perceive the field, position themselves within it, and act in ways that are “reasonable” given its structure. These dispositions are historically formed through education, training, and career trajectories, and they are reproduced as newcomers internalise the existing norms and practices of the field **Bourdieu 2004; Schirone 2023**. We can therefore distinguish between a **disciplinary habitus**, shared by members of a broad disciplinary area (such as Public Administration), and more specific **particular habitus**, linked to subfields, institutional trajectories, or positions in the field. The result is a self-reinforcing dynamic: the field’s structure shapes the habitus of its agents, and the aggregated practices of these agents reproduce, adjust, or contest that structure.
From this perspective, **disciplines** are not something fundamentally different from fields, but rather **fields that have undergone a strong process of institutionalisation**. As Bourdieu argues, a discipline is a scientific field that has formalised its means of knowledge production and reproduction: it has established associations, conferences, journals, degree programmes, and the right to appoint official representatives and gatekeepers **Bourdieu 2004; Schirone 2023**. Disciplines are thus fields with a high degree of institutional consolidation and relative autonomy, yet always embedded in broader power structures and broader fields (e.g. the academic field, the state, or the policy arena). In the case of _Public Administration_, this dual nature is particularly salient: it is both an academic field, with its own journals and associations, and a practice-oriented domain historically tied to the state and public sector reform.
Adopting this Bourdieusian definition has two important implications for this project. First, it emphasises that the boundaries of _Public Administration_ as a field are **historical and contested**, rather than natural or self-evident. What is recognised as “Public Administration research” at a given moment reflects past and present struggles over scientific authority, institutional recognition, and practical relevance. Second, it opens to a series of reflections beyond the pure and irenic image of the scientific community, with the intent of building a database capable of bringing out the asymmetrical power relations between different agents and the unequal distribution of scientific authority and its causes, such as gender and race inequalities (**Liu et al. 2023; Pandey et al. 2023 Smith et al. 2025 Sugimoto and Larivière 2023**), or uneven geographical productivity **Finocchi et al. 2025**.

- The Tension Between Conceptualisation and Operationalisation and consequences
While conceptual definitions clarify what fields are, bibliometric research requires that fields also be **operationalised**—translated into measurable units. As **Sugimoto and Weingart 2015** emphasise, there is an inherent, perhaps unavoidable tension between conceptualisations and operationalisations. Conceptual definitions capture nuance, complexity, and multidimensionality, whereas operational definitions impose simplifications necessary for empirical analysis. Every operational boundary—whether based on journals, departments, keywords, or networks—reduces the complexity of real scholarly practice and risks introducing distortions or reproducing the existing asymmetrical power relations. Nonetheless, operational definitions are indispensable for reproducibility, comparability, and systematic analysis. The key is to adopt them while fully acknowledging their limitations and their status as heuristic devices rather than definitive representations of disciplinary reality.
Operationalising a field has significant consequences. Classifying journals, authors, or ideas into a field allocates symbolic capital and can shape perceptions of legitimacy and status. It affects which research is recognised, funded, rewarded, or deemed peripheral. In fields such as Public Administration, which historically oscillate between academic inquiry and practitioner-oriented knowledge, defining boundaries also shapes the balance between scholarly autonomy and practical relevance. Moreover, operational boundaries are used in evaluation systems, rankings, and hiring practices; they influence how careers develop and how scientific authority is distributed. Defining something as “inside” or “outside” a field is therefore not a neutral act but part of the broader social structure and power dynamics within the scientific domain—precisely the dynamics Bourdieu’s theory brings to light.

- Operationalising Fields for Bibliometric Analysis
Because conceptual definitions of scientific fields are inherently multidimensional and open to contestation, bibliometric research requires clear, transparent **operational boundaries** that allow systematic analysis. As discussed by **Sugimoto and Weingart 2015**, operationalisation strategies typically fall into three broad families: those based on **publications**, **people**, and **ideas**. Among these, **publication-based approaches—especially those using journals—have become the dominant and most widely accepted method** in the science-of-science literature **Sugimoto and Larivière 2018**.
This predominance is rooted in the centrality of journals to the academic communication system. Journals are not simply repositories of papers; they are institutionalised channels through which fields structure their intellectual identity, articulate standards of legitimacy, and reproduce their social order. As **Schirone 2024**** argues, journals crystallise the cognitive and social organisation of fields, making them especially powerful proxies for delineating disciplinary boundaries.
Methodologically, journal classifications such as the Journal Citation Reports (JCR) offer stable, replicable structures for organising the scientific literature. They have a long history in information retrieval and remain widely used despite acknowledged limitations. Indeed, **Boyack and Klavans 2011** warn that journal-level classifications should be employed cautiously for evaluative purposes, but they nevertheless recognise them among the best available field-delineation tools in large-scale quantitative research.
Given this theoretical and empirical foundation, operationalising the Public Administration field through journal classifications is not merely pragmatic but **conceptually coherent**, aligning with established norms in scientometrics and with the sociological understanding of fields as institutionalised spaces of communication, authority, and competition.
Indeed, from a Bourdieusian perspective, journals function as **a suitable proxy to understand the struggle for scientific authority**, embedding within them the hierarchies, struggles, and historical trajectories of a scientific field. Their symbolic capital—whether expressed through citation metrics, reputational prestige, selectivity, or editorial lineage—reflects the accumulated recognition of the field’s agents over time. Journals thus embody both **cultural capital** (the codified knowledge, theories, and methods deemed legitimate) and **social capital** (the networks, collaborations, and institutional affiliations circulating through editorial boards, reviewers, and authors).
Journals therefore play a **gatekeeping role**. Editors, editorial boards, and reviewers collectively exercise scientific authority by validating some contributions and excluding others. In Public Administration, **Liu et al. 2024** show that this gatekeeping function shapes the internationalisation of the field, with journals acting as the main vehicles through which knowledge travels globally. Lasswell’s communication model, used by Liu et al. to assess internationality, reinforces that every stage of journal-mediated communication—who sends knowledge, how it is encoded, through what channel, to whom, and with what effects—actively participates in shaping the field’s intellectual and social contours. In addition, journals also serve as instruments of **field conservation**. As Bourdieu describes, they tend to consecrate authors aligned with the dominant principles of the field’s “official science” while discouraging heterodox submissions. Thus, journals stabilise field boundaries, perpetuate established power structures, and contribute to the reproduction of the scientific order. This role of journals comes as a fundamental feature to discover all the asymmetrical power relations existing, how they have formed, and suggesting more equitable distributional policies. 
To summarise, the focus on **journals and journal classifications** as central operational tools: journals are not neutral containers, but material artefacts where symbolic capital is accumulated, where habitus is reproduced, and where the field’s cognitive and social structures become visible and measurable. On this basis, it becomes theoretically coherent to use established journal categories—such as the **JCR SSCI and ESCI Public Administration classification**—to operationalise the field for bibliometric analysis, while fully acknowledging the simplifications and exclusions that this entails.
First, JCR categories represent **institutionalised recognitions of symbolic capital**. Inclusion in the PA category is not arbitrary: it reflects the accumulated prestige, editorial quality, peer-review legitimacy, and communicative centrality of journals historically recognised by the field. From a Bourdieusian standpoint, the JCR PA category embodies the field’s **objectified structure of capital**, capturing the journals that occupy positions of authority within its intellectual and social space.
Second, JCR remains the most widely used and internationally standardised classification system in scientometrics **Sugimoto and Weingart 2015**. It provides stable and transparent boundaries that facilitate comparison across studies and ensure replicability. Although imperfect, it remains the most practical and credible “convenient bucket” for delineating fields in quantitative analysis. Relying on JCR thus aligns with established methodological practice in the science-of-science literature **Boyack and Klavans 2011**.
Third, using the SSCI and ESCI categories together captures both **established and emerging journals**, allowing the operational definition of Public Administration to reflect the evolving and increasingly diversified structure of the field. Emerging journals often correspond to new research communities, methodological innovations, or shifts in global participation, making their inclusion crucial for a contemporary analysis of field structures.
Fourth, this decision is strongly supported by recent empirical work in Public Administration. Indeed, many bibliometrics analyses focused on the PA field have used the JCR Public Administration category, or other journal-based field delineation, for identify the Public Administration field (**Corley and Sabharwal 2010; Hattke and Vogel 2023; LePere-Schoop and Nesbit 2023; Ma and Cheng 2025 Pandey et al. 2023; Smith et al. 2025; Wagner and Raadschelders 2025**)
- **Coverage period**
The retrieval will cover the period 1980–2024. Bibliographic metadata tend to become substantially more reliable from the 1980s onward, as noted by Sugimoto and Larivière (2018). The year 2025 will be excluded to mitigate the risk of recency-related inaccuracies in indexing, metadata propagation, or submission delays, whether originating from editors or from OpenAlex.
- **Document types**
Through the OpenAlex API it is possible to select among several work types (e.g., article, book chapter, dataset, dissertation, book, review, paratext, report, editorial, software, standard, and others).
For consistency and comparability, this project will retrieve only works labelled as “articles”.
- **Language selection**
The JCR SSCI and ESCI categories in Public Administration are predominantly English-language journals. However, a small number of journals publish in Spanish or French. In line with the Leiden Manifesto’s recommendations to value high-quality non-English scholarship, these journals will also be included. No explicit language filter will therefore be applied at the retrieval stage.
- **Metadata selection**
OpenAlex is organised around entities (e.g., Works, Authors, Institutions). This initial retrieval will focus exclusively on the Work entity. The following metadata fields will be retrieved:
- **Work object fields**
1. abstract_inverted_index – OpenAlex stores abstracts as inverted indices due to copyright constraints.
2. biblio – volume, issue, first page, and last page.
3. cited_by_count – citation count, which will be updated periodically in future updates.
4. doi – the DOI of the published version (OpenAlex prioritises the version of record when multiple DOIs exist).
5. grants – funder information and award identifiers where available. OpenAlex notes that grant metadata are incomplete across the database.
6. id – the OpenAlex Work ID; essential for downstream SQL database construction.
7. is_paratext – identifies front matter, back matter, editorials, etc. Works flagged as paratext will later be excluded during data cleaning.
8. language – based on metadata language, not full-text language; discrepancies may occur.
9. open_access – includes OA status, licence (if available), and associated OA URLs.
10. publication_year.
11. referenced_works – list of OpenAlex Work IDs cited by the article.
12. title.
13. type – expected to be “journal-article” for all retrieved records.
14. is_oa – boolean indicator of OA status.
15. oa_status – more detailed OA categorisation (gold, green, hybrid, bronze, closed).
16. oa_url – link to publisher or repository full text where available.
- **Authorship object fields**
Within the Work object, the authorships list provides dehydrated metadata about authors and institutions. The following elements will be retrieved:
17. author – dehydrated Author object including the OpenAlex Author ID, display name, and ORCID. These IDs will form the basis of subsequent retrievals of full Author objects during the data enrichment phase.
18. countries – inferred country information based on parsed affiliation strings; this may sometimes appear even when no institution ID is present.
19. institutions – dehydrated Institution objects indicating the affiliation(s) reported for each author in the context of that specific work. These IDs will be used for subsequent retrievals of full Institution objects during the data enrichment phase

### Data retrieval
Records will be retrieved at the journal level, using OpenAlex Source IDs to ensure precise identification.
The full list of Public Administration journals indexed in the JCR SSCI and ESCI categories will be exported (file: PA_JCR_SSCI_ESCI.xlsx).
Corresponding OpenAlex Source IDs will be identified and used within the query (Python script: openalex_records_retrieval.py) to prevent the retrieval of records from journals with similar or ambiguous titles.
The retrieval script will query all journal IDs listed in PA_JCR_SSCI_ESCI.xlsx, applying the filters and metadata selections outlined in this section. The final, validated version of the script will be released on GitHub after debugging.

- **Retrieval method**
Python script: openalex_records_retrieval.py (to be released on GitHub when finalised).
Queries built around OpenAlex Source IDs corresponding to the PA journal set.
API procedures documented in docs/methodology.md.
Raw data stored in data/raw/ as JSONL and CSV exports.

- **Planned outputs**
Complete metadata CSV  file for all retrieved records.
A public sample dataset (first 100 records) archived on Zenodo.
A log file tracking query execution, rate-limit handling, and any retrieval anomalies.
Summary statistics describing retrieval coverage (e.g., number of records per journal, missing fields, annual distribution).
